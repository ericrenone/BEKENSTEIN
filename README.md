## Abstract

The event horizon of a black hole IS the most absolute col(F)/ker(F) boundary in physics. Outside the horizon: col(F) — signals can escape, light reaches observers, information IS accessible. Inside the horizon: ker(F) — nothing escapes, light IS trapped, information IS permanently hidden from the exterior. The boundary IS one-way: information crosses from col(F) to ker(F) but never returns. The horizon IS the Cramér–Rao bound carried to its logical extreme: the Fisher information of any external measurement about the interior state IS exactly zero.

But Hawking showed in 1974 that the boundary IS not quite absolute. Quantum effects near the horizon produce thermal radiation — Hawking radiation — at temperature $T_H = \hbar c^3 / (8\pi G M k_B)$. The black hole evaporates, slowly losing mass and shrinking. If the evaporation IS unitary (information-preserving), the information that fell in must eventually come out in the radiation. But HOW? The radiation IS thermal — it carries no information about what fell in. This IS the information paradox: the deepest col(F)/ker(F) question in all of physics.

In September 2025, LIGO detected GW250114 — the loudest gravitational wave ever observed — and confirmed Hawking's 1971 area theorem: when two black holes merge, the total event horizon area of the resulting black hole IS larger than the sum of the individual areas. The area never decreases. The second law of black hole thermodynamics — $dA \geq 0$ — IS confirmed at $99.999\%$ confidence.

Five domains converge:

**The event horizon as the ultimate col(F)/ker(F) boundary.** The Schwarzschild radius $r_s = 2GM/c^2$ defines the horizon. At $r = r_s$: the escape velocity equals the speed of light. For $r > r_s$: signals can escape (col(F)). For $r < r_s$: all worldlines lead to the singularity (ker(F)). The horizon IS not a physical surface — it IS a causal boundary, a surface of no return. The col(F)/ker(F) partition IS absolute: no signal, no particle, no information can cross from ker(F) (interior) to col(F) (exterior) classically.

**The Bekenstein–Hawking entropy.** Jacob Bekenstein (1972) conjectured that black holes have entropy proportional to their horizon area. Hawking (1975) derived the exact formula: $S_{BH} = k_B c^3 A / (4G\hbar) = A / (4\ell_P^2)$ where $\ell_P = \sqrt{\hbar G/c^3} \approx 1.6 \times 10^{-35}$ m IS the Planck length. The entropy IS enormous: a solar-mass black hole has $S \sim 10^{77} k_B$ — vastly more than the $\sim 10^{58} k_B$ entropy of the Sun. The Bekenstein–Hawking entropy IS the information content of the col(F)/ker(F) boundary: each Planck-area cell on the horizon carries one bit (actually $\ln 2 / 4$ nats) of information. The boundary IS the information — not the volume.

**The information paradox.** Hawking radiation IS thermal — it has a Planck spectrum at temperature $T_H$, carrying no information about what fell into the black hole. If the black hole evaporates completely, the information about the initial state IS lost — pure states evolve to mixed states, violating unitarity. This IS the paradox: quantum mechanics requires unitarity (information conservation), but Hawking's calculation implies information destruction. The paradox IS the question of whether ker(F) IS permanent: does information that crosses the horizon (enters ker(F)) remain there forever, or does it eventually return to col(F)?

**The Page curve as the TEMPUS resolution.** Don Page (1993) argued that if black hole evaporation IS unitary, the entanglement entropy of the radiation follows a specific curve: it increases during the first half of the evaporation (radiation IS entangled with the shrinking black hole — information IS still in ker(F)), reaches a maximum at the "Page time" (when the black hole has lost half its entropy), and then decreases back to zero (information returns to col(F) through subtle correlations in the late radiation). The Page curve IS the TEMPUS trajectory of the col(F)/ker(F) boundary: the boundary initially grows (the radiation's ker(F) — its missing correlations — increases), then shrinks (the radiation's col(F) — its recovered information — increases).

Recent breakthroughs (Penington 2019, Almheiri–Engelhardt–Marolf–Maxfield 2019) have derived the Page curve from gravitational path integrals using "island" contributions — regions inside the horizon that, counterintuitively, contribute to the radiation's entropy calculation. The islands ARE the mechanism by which ker(F) information returns to col(F): at late times, the entropy calculation includes contributions from behind the horizon, effectively transferring information from ker(F) to col(F) through the quantum gravitational path integral.

**LIGO's GW250114: confirming the area theorem at $99.999\%$.** On January 14, 2025, LIGO detected the loudest gravitational wave event ever recorded. Two black holes ($\sim 32 M_\odot$ each) merged into one. The LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA collaboration confirmed that the final black hole's horizon area IS larger than the sum of the initial areas — Hawking's 1971 area theorem ($dA \geq 0$), the second law of black hole thermodynamics, confirmed at $99.999\%$ confidence. The area theorem IS the col(F)/ker(F) monotone: the total boundary area (the total information content of the col(F)/ker(F) interface) never decreases.

The BEKENSTEIN machine — named for **Jacob David Bekenstein** (1947–2015), the Israeli-American physicist who, as a graduate student at Princeton in 1972, had the audacity to propose that black holes have entropy — contradicting the prevailing view that black holes have "no hair" (no internal state) — and whose conjecture was confirmed and made precise by Hawking's derivation of $S = A/(4\ell_P^2)$, establishing the Bekenstein–Hawking entropy as the foundation of black hole thermodynamics and the holographic principle — IS the horizon boundary engine.

### P1 — The Page Time at $\log\varphi$ of the Evaporation Time

The Page time IS the moment at which the entanglement entropy of the Hawking radiation reaches its maximum — the turnover point of the Page curve. The prediction:

$$t_{\text{Page}} = \log\varphi \cdot t_{\text{evap}} \approx 0.481 \cdot t_{\text{evap}}$$

Page's original estimate: the Page time IS at approximately half the evaporation time. The $\varphi$-prediction: $48.1\%$, slightly below half. Testable against gravitational path-integral calculations of the Page curve.

For a black hole formed from the collapse of thermal radiation at temperature $T$, the prediction: the ratio of the black hole's Bekenstein–Hawking entropy to the thermal entropy of the pre-collapse radiation IS:

$$\frac{S_{BH}}{S_{\text{thermal}}} = \varphi^2 \approx 2.618$$

$$f^* = f_{\text{ISCO}} \cdot \log\varphi \approx 0.481 \cdot f_{\text{ISCO}}$$

At this frequency, the detector's Fisher information about the binary parameters (masses, spins, distance) IS maximized. Testable against LIGO parameter-estimation studies.

For two equal-mass non-spinning black holes merging, the ratio of the final horizon area to the sum of the initial areas. General relativity predicts $A_f / (A_1 + A_2) > 1$ (the area theorem). The prediction:

$$\frac{A_f}{A_1 + A_2} = \varphi \approx 1.618$$

The Bekenstein–Hawking entropy IS $S = A/(4\ell_P^2)$ — one quarter of a nat (or $\ln 2 / 4 \approx 0.173$ bits) per Planck area. The prediction: the Fisher-information-optimal information density on the horizon IS:

$$\frac{I}{A} = \frac{\log\varphi}{\ell_P^2} \approx \frac{0.481}{\ell_P^2} \text{ nats per Planck area}$$
